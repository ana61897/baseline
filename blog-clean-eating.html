The Truth About "Clean Eating" — There's No Such Thing

5 min read
🥦

"Clean eating" entered the wellness lexicon sometime in the mid-2000s, and it did something remarkably effective for a phrase with no formal definition: it made food a matter of virtue. Ask a nutrition scientist for the clinical definition of a "clean" food and you'll get a shrug — not because the science is unsettled, but because there isn't one. That's not an oversight. It's the entire appeal. A label with no fixed meaning can mean whatever the person using it needs it to mean that week.

Where the Line Actually Comes From

In practice, "clean" usually gets mapped onto "unprocessed," and "dirty" onto "processed." The problem is that processing itself isn't a meaningful predictor of anything nutritionally. Canning, freezing, fortification, and pasteurization are all forms of processing, and all of them can make food safer, more nutritious, or more accessible — not less.

Frozen vegetables are picked and flash-frozen at peak ripeness, which often means they retain more vitamin C than "fresh" produce that spent a week in transit and another few days in your crisper drawer. Canned beans and canned tomatoes are nutritionally comparable to their fresh counterparts, minus the sodium you can rinse off in thirty seconds. None of that fits neatly into a clean-versus-dirty framework, because the framework was never built from nutrition science in the first place.

The Real Cost of the Framework

Moralizing food — good versus bad, clean versus dirty — isn't just imprecise, it's been linked in the research literature to disordered eating patterns. Orthorexia, an unofficial but increasingly recognized term for an obsessive fixation on the purity of one's diet, tends to take root in exactly this kind of black-and-white food language. That doesn't mean everyone who says "eating clean" is at risk. It means the framework itself doesn't have a ceiling — there's always a cleaner version of clean, and that's a genuinely uncomfortable place to live.

Worth knowing: Orthorexia isn't about eating too little food — it's about the psychological toll of rigid, rule-based eating focused on purity rather than nutrition. It's not yet a formal diagnosis in the DSM, but clinicians increasingly recognize it as a real and distinct pattern, separate from other eating disorders.

What Actually Predicts Outcomes

None of the research on long-term health outcomes organizes itself around "clean" versus "dirty." What it organizes around is considerably less exciting: nutrient density, fiber intake, protein adequacy, and — more than any single food choice — the consistency of your overall pattern over months and years, not the purity of any one plate.

Nobody has ever gotten measurably healthier from feeling morally superior about a salad. They've gotten healthier from eating enough protein, enough fiber, and roughly the right number of calories for their goals, consistently, for a long time. That's a much less Instagrammable answer, but it's the one the evidence actually supports.

Bottom Line

Skip the clean-versus-dirty label entirely and ask three more useful questions instead: Does this fit my goals today? Is my overall pattern working over weeks, not meals? Am I getting enough of what actually matters — protein, fiber, and micronutrients? Food doesn't need a purity test. It needs a plan.
